### Trademarks

A trademark is a name, word, symbol, or design that identifies the source of a product or service. The difference between a registered trademark and a common law trademark is the difference between a property right and a usage right, and confusing the two has real consequences.

#### Common Law Trademark: TM

A common law trademark is a trademark that has not been registered with the United States Patent and Trademark Office, USPTO, or the equivalent in another jurisdiction. You acquire common law trademark rights simply by using the mark in commerce. No filing, no fee, no registration.

The symbol for a common law trademark is the TM symbol, written as TM or (TM). You use TM to put the public on notice that you claim trademark rights in the mark, even though you have not registered it.

The requirements for common law trademark protection:

1. **Use in commerce.** You must actually use the mark in the sale of goods or the provision of services. A mark that exists only on a business card but is not used in commerce does not acquire common law rights.
2. **Distinctiveness.** The mark must be distinctive, not generic. You cannot trademark the word "startup" for a startup company. You can trademark "AStartup" for a specific software product.
3. **Geographic scope.** Common law rights are limited to the geographic area where you actually use the mark. If you use "AStartup" in Oregon, you have common law rights in Oregon. You do not have rights in Texas until you use the mark in Texas.

The risk: because common law rights are not registered, they are not in the public record. Someone else can register the same mark in a different jurisdiction, and you will have to litigate to prove that you were using it first. The TM symbol is your public notice, but it is not legal proof. The proof is your sales records, your marketing materials, your domain registration, your app store listing: the documentation that shows when and where you started using the mark.

#### Registered Trademark: R

A registered trademark is a trademark that has been registered with the USPTO or the equivalent in another jurisdiction. The registration process involves a filing, a search to ensure the mark is not already registered, a examination by a trademark examiner, and a publication period during which anyone can oppose the registration.

The symbol for a registered trademark is the R symbol, written as R or (R). You use R only for marks that are actually registered. Using R for a mark that is not registered is a misrepresentation and can result in fines.

The advantages of registration:

1. **Nationwide rights.** A federal registration gives you rights across the entire country, not just the area where you use the mark.
2. **Public record.** The registration is in the USPTO database. Anyone searching for available marks will find yours, which deters others from registering the same or a confusingly similar mark.
3. **Legal presumption.** In a infringement lawsuit, a registered trademark is presumed valid. The burden is on the challenger to prove the registration should be cancelled. A common law trademark has no such presumption; you must prove your rights from scratch.
4. **Statutory remedies.** A registered trademark owner can recover profits, damages, and attorney's fees in an infringement lawsuit. A common law trademark owner can only recover actual damages.

#### Which One Do You Use?

The rule is simple:

* If the mark is registered with the USPTO, use R.
* If the mark is not registered, use TM.
* If the mark is in the process of being registered but has not been approved yet, use TM. Do not use R until the registration is final.

The Captain lost a grant once because a panel member called out the use of TM when R was the appropriate symbol. The mark was registered, and using TM instead of R signaled to the panel that the founder did not understand the difference. The fix: know which marks are registered and which are not, and use the correct symbol every time.

For a solo founder: register your primary brand name. The filing fee is a few hundred dollars, the process takes eight to twelve months, and the protection is worth it. Use TM for secondary marks, product names, and features that are not yet registered. Use R only for the marks that are actually on the USPTO register.

#### Agent and Trademark

When an AI agent is generating marketing copy, website content, or product documentation, the agent must use the correct trademark symbol. The rule goes in the AGENTS.md:

```markdown
## Trademark Usage

* AStartup (R) — registered trademark, use R symbol
* Astartup Toolkit (TM) — common law trademark, use TM symbol
* Kabuki Starship (TM) — common law trademark, use TM symbol
* AStarship (TM) — common law trademark, use TM symbol

The agent must use the correct symbol in all generated content.
Do not use R for unregistered marks. Do not omit the symbol entirely.
```

This is not legal advice. This is the operational rule for keeping the symbols consistent across the content that agents generate. For legal questions about trademark registration, infringement, or enforcement, consult an attorney.
