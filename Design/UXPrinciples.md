### UX Principles

Dan Brown's eight principles concern information architecture: how content is structured, classified, and found. They are useful design heuristics, not a complete UX standard or universal numerical limits. The summaries below paraphrase his 2010 article.[13]

#### Eight information-architecture principles

| Principle | Meaning | A startup application to test |
|---|---|---|
| Objects | Treat content as having attributes, behavior, and a lifecycle. | Define a document's owner, status, relationships, and update policy. |
| Choices | Offer meaningful choices focused on the current task. | Group competing actions so users can choose the next step. |
| Disclosure | Show enough information to understand what lies deeper. | Use a useful preview before a detailed document, without hiding material costs or risks. |
| Exemplars | Explain categories by showing examples of their contents. | Show representative records beside an unfamiliar category name. |
| Front doors | Design for arrival on pages other than the home page. | Test a direct link to a pricing, documentation, or support page. |
| Multiple classification | Support more than one useful way of browsing content. | Offer task-based and concept-based paths when users need both. |
| Focused navigation | Keep each navigation scheme coherent rather than mixing unrelated kinds of choices. | Separate product tasks from account administration where this helps users. |
| Growth | Expect content to expand and plan the structure accordingly. | Test the organization with plausible future content, not just today's small sample. |

Brown's front-door assumption is a design prompt, not a measured traffic statistic for your startup. These principles do not prescribe exactly three plans, five to seven navigation items, flat hierarchies, or a two-minute completion target for every task.

#### Verify with users

Select a real user task, entry point, baseline, and success criterion. Observe where users hesitate, choose an unintended route, or need help. Record task completion, errors, time where relevant, and accessibility barriers. Compare a proposed change against that evidence, not against how attractive a generated mockup looks.

An MVP still needs the disclosures, safety, and accessibility required for its use. Agree on the appropriate checks with the owner and specialists; this chapter is not a compliance determination.

#### Agents and local models

Ask a model to identify content types, navigation ambiguities, or missing context using these principles. Require a page/path and a proposed check for each finding. The output is a design hypothesis until observation or testing supports it. It is not a substitute for customer research.
